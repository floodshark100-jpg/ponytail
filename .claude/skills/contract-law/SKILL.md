# Contract Law

General legal information, not legal advice. Law differs by jurisdiction
(US state law, UK, EU civil law and others). Always ask which governing law
applies, or find it in the contract. Say plainly when something needs a
licensed lawyer: litigation, large sums, employment disputes, real estate,
anything already in dispute, or any deadline that is about to run out.

## 1. Is there a contract at all?

Check each element. If one is missing, the contract may be void or voidable.

| Element | Test |
|---|---|
| Offer | Definite terms, meant to be binding (an ad is usually an invitation to treat) |
| Acceptance | Matches the offer exactly. Changed terms are a counter-offer (mirror-image rule; UCC 2-207 relaxes this for sales of goods) |
| Consideration | Each side gives something of value. Past consideration does not count. Civil-law systems do not require it |
| Intention | Intent to be legally bound. Presumed in business deals, not in family or social ones |
| Capacity | Not a minor, not mentally incapacitated, and the signer has authority to bind the company |
| Legality | The purpose is lawful and not against public policy |
| Formalities | Some contracts must be in writing: land, guarantees, deals that cannot be performed within a year, US sales of goods of $500 or more (statute of frauds), deeds |

Grounds to void or rescind: misrepresentation, fraud, duress, undue
influence, unconscionability, mutual mistake.

## 2. Review checklist (go through these in order)

1. **Parties.** Correct legal entity names, signatory authority, affiliates.
2. **Term and renewal.** Start date, end date, auto-renewal and the notice window to stop it.
3. **Scope / deliverables.** Specific enough to tell whether someone is in breach. Acceptance criteria.
4. **Payment.** Amount, currency, due date, late interest, price escalation, taxes, set-off.
5. **Termination.** For convenience or only for cause? Cure period, notice, what survives termination.
6. **Liability cap.** The cap amount (often 12 months of fees). What is carved out of the cap (IP, confidentiality, indemnity, gross negligence, wilful misconduct).
7. **Exclusion of consequential damages.** Lost profits, lost data. Does it apply to both sides?
8. **Indemnities.** Who covers which third-party claims, defence control, whether the cap applies.
9. **Warranties and disclaimers.** Express warranties, "as is", fitness for purpose, merchantability.
10. **IP.** Who owns pre-existing IP and new work, licence scope, work-for-hire, moral rights.
11. **Confidentiality.** Definition, exclusions, how long it lasts, return or destruction of information.
12. **Data protection.** GDPR/CCPA roles (controller or processor), a DPA, breach notice, data transfers.
13. **Non-compete / non-solicit.** Often unenforceable or limited (California, FTC rules, UK reasonableness test).
14. **Assignment and change of control.** Whether consent is needed.
15. **Force majeure.** Listed events, notice, right to terminate after a long delay.
16. **Governing law, venue, dispute resolution.** Courts or arbitration, class-action waiver, jury waiver.
17. **Boilerplate.** Entire agreement, order of precedence, amendments only in writing, severability, notices, counterparts and e-signature.

For each issue, report: the clause, the risk to the user, and a suggested
redline. Point out what is **missing**, not only what is there.

## 3. Interpretation rules

- Plain meaning first. Read the contract as a whole, and specific terms beat general ones.
- Contra proferentem: ambiguity is read against the party that drafted the clause.
- Defined terms control. Check every capitalised term against its definition.
- Parol evidence: an entire-agreement clause usually shuts out earlier oral promises.
- Course of dealing and trade usage can fill gaps.
- "Shall" means an obligation, "may" means discretion. "Best efforts" is a higher bar than "reasonable efforts", which is higher than "commercially reasonable efforts" (exact meaning varies by jurisdiction).

## 4. Breach and remedies

- **Material breach** lets the other side terminate and claim damages. A **minor** breach only gives damages.
- **Anticipatory repudiation**: one side clearly says it will not perform.
- **Damages**: expectation (the default), reliance, restitution. Limits: foreseeability (*Hadley v Baxendale*), the duty to mitigate, causation.
- **Liquidated damages** are enforceable if they are a genuine pre-estimate. They fail if they are a penalty.
- **Equitable remedies**: specific performance (unique goods, land), injunction, rescission.
- **Limitation periods** vary. Examples: UK 6 years (12 for a deed). US states usually 3–6 years for written contracts, UCC 4 years.

## 5. How to answer

- Quote the exact clause text you rely on. Never paraphrase a clause into meaning something it does not say.
- State your assumptions about jurisdiction. Name the deal type and which side the user is on.
- Lead with the top three risks, then the full checklist.
- Do not invent statutes, cases or citations. If you are unsure, say so and tell the user to verify.
- End with: "This is general information, not legal advice. Have a qualified lawyer in [jurisdiction] review it before you sign."
